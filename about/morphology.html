Morphology

A galaxy's morphology, whether it is an extended, rotationally supported disc or a compact, dispersion-dominated spheroid, is not a fixed label but the accumulated record of its assembly. In-situ star formation within a settled, gas-rich disc tends to build up size and preserve angular momentum; mergers redistribute angular momentum and can partially or fully transform a disc into a spheroid; and quenching, whatever mechanism shuts off star formation, freezes a galaxy's structure at whatever state it had reached, often before the disc is fully destroyed. A galaxy's size and shape therefore record its merger history and its quenching history superimposed on one another.

Semi-analytic models require simple, physically motivated prescriptions for how size and structure respond to a merger or to quenching itself, but these prescriptions are typically calibrated against idealised two-body simulations or basic energy-conservation arguments rather than tested against a realistic, cosmological population of mergers and quenching events. My work tests these prescriptions directly against full hydrodynamical simulations, quantifying where the standard assumptions hold and where they break down.

  • Do standard semi-analytic prescriptions for merger-driven size growth and disc destruction survive contact with a realistic, cosmological population of mergers?
  • How does quenching reshape a galaxy's structure, independent of mergers?
  • Is a galaxy's structure mainly a cause of its quenching pathway, or mainly a consequence of it?
