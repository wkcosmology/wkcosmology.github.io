Galaxy Morphology

Galaxies in the local Universe fall into a remarkably orderly pattern on the Hubble tuning-fork diagram, which classifies them morphologically as elliptical, lenticular or spiral, with the residual population designated irregular. Ellipticals occupy the left of the diagram, lenticulars sit at the junction, and the spirals divide into two branches according to the presence or absence of a central bar. More remarkable still is that this purely morphological classification correlates strongly with the physical properties of galaxies. Spirals are typically bluer, gas-rich, rotationally supported, and preferentially found in sparse environments, whereas ellipticals are redder, gas-poor, pressure-supported, and preferentially located in dense environments. Establishing the origin of the Hubble sequence, and how it relates to the stellar and gaseous content of galaxies, is therefore a key question in astrophysics.

My approach is to decompose this diversity into a small set of characteristics, ordered by how much of the variance in galaxy structure each one captures, in the spirit of a principal component analysis. The primary characteristic is galaxy size. Sizes are far easier to increase than to decrease: stars are collisionless, so a stellar system has few channels through which to dissipate orbital energy and contract. Growth, by contrast, proceeds in two ways. A galaxy can form new stars with higher angular momentum or higher orbital energy, on orbits that reach further out and so enlarge the galaxy from within; or it can merge with another galaxy, converting orbital energy into the internal motions of the remnant. The secondary characteristics are the three-dimensional shape, running from prolate to oblate, and the kinematic state, running from rotation-supported to dispersion-supported. These two are not independent: prolate galaxies are typically dispersion-supported ellipticals, and oblate galaxies typically rotation-supported discs. The notable exception is the bar, which is prolate in shape yet rotation-supported, and that mismatch is much of what makes bars such an interesting structure to study.

Modern hydrodynamical galaxy formation simulations produce morphologies that broadly resemble real galaxies, but each one emerges from many coupled processes acting at once, and reproducing the observed mix of morphological types does not by itself reveal which process made a given galaxy what it is. Semi-analytic models make the opposite trade. Rather than following the gas and stellar dynamics directly, they describe each transformation with an explicit physical prescription, and predict how the population as a whole responds: the distribution of sizes, the split between disc and bulge, the fraction of galaxies of each morphological type. Every outcome can therefore be traced back to the prescription that produced it. My approach is to combine the two. I extract the effective prescriptions from the simulations, where the dynamics is resolved and each transformation can be followed galaxy by galaxy, and implement them in a semi-analytic model, whose transparency allows the dominant driver of each morphological characteristic to be identified. The resulting predictions can then be tested against observations, both in the distribution of morphologies and in how morphology correlates with other galaxy properties. The key questions I address are:

  • What sets the Hubble sequence, and how does it emerge across cosmic time?
  • To what extent is galaxy morphology determined by the assembly history of the host halo?
  • What physical mechanisms link morphology to the star formation and quenching of galaxies?
