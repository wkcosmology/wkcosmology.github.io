Galaxy Formation & Evolution

From our Earth, to our Galaxy, to the whole Universe

Our home, the Earth, is one of eight planets orbiting the Sun. The Sun is one of a few hundred billion stars in our own galaxy, the Milky Way, and the Milky Way is one of billions of galaxies in the observable Universe. Beyond what we can observe, space may extend far further still — perhaps without end — but we can neither see it nor communicate with it, because light travels at a finite speed. That same limit is also a gift: the distant galaxies we see today appear as they were when the Universe was young, since their light was sent out millions or billions of years ago and is only now reaching us. By looking far away with great telescopes such as Hubble and JWST, we can look back through the entire history of the Universe.

Most of the visible matter in the Universe is bound up in galaxies, each made of millions to billions of shining stars. Galaxies come in a remarkable variety of shapes: some are flat like a plate, the disc galaxies; some resemble a sombrero, a disc with a bright central bulge; some are nothing but bulge; and others are so irregular and tangled that astronomers long ago gave up trying to classify them neatly. They differ in colour too — bluer where new stars are still forming, redder where star formation has largely ceased.

Understanding how galaxies follow such different paths to arrive at the shapes and colours we see today is one of the great open questions in astronomy. It is the question I am pursuing.

How galaxies form and evolve?

Baryon cycle

Decades of observation with great telescopes, together with theoretical work on paper and on computers, have assembled a general picture of how galaxies form and evolve. It begins with the collapse of dark matter haloes, whose deep gravitational potential wells draw in the surrounding baryons. As the gas falls inward, gravitational potential energy is converted into kinetic energy and the gas is heated. Collisions between atoms then lift electrons into higher energy levels, and the photons emitted as those electrons decay carry energy out of the system, allowing the gas to cool and settle towards the centre of the halo. Dark matter has no such channel — it neither collides nor radiates — so it cannot continue to collapse, and instead remains in a virialised state.

The cooled gas contracts further, reaching higher and higher densities until clouds become unstable and fragment, and at last the temperatures and pressures in their cores are enough to ignite nuclear fusion: stars are born. Inside those stars, heavier elements are forged, and stellar winds carry the freshly synthesised metals back out into the surrounding gas. Enriched in this way, the gas goes on to form new generations of stars, and the cycle continues. The most massive stars live only briefly before dying in catastrophic explosions — supernovae — which drive gas and metals outward, sometimes clear out of the galaxy altogether, though much of it eventually rains back down. This continual cycle of gas — inflow, star formation, enrichment, and outflow — is known as the baryon cycle, and it is the foundation of our framework for galaxy formation.

Galaxy morphology

Galaxies also differ in morphology and size, and the origins of that variety are more tangled. Part of the answer is rotation: a galaxy forms from gas drawn out of a large region of space that already carries a small amount of angular momentum, imparted by the tidal torques of its neighbours in the early Universe. As that gas collapses, its angular velocity grows — the same principle that makes a figure skater spin faster when they pull in their arms, since angular momentum is conserved. The resulting rotation supports the gas against further collapse and settles it into a disc, which is precisely what we observe: disc galaxies rotate like frisbees. But gas can only dissipate energy while it remains gas. Once it has been converted into stars, those stars behave much like dark matter, with no channel to radiate their orbital energy away, so a galaxy that never acquired much angular momentum is instead supported by the random motions of its stars, as a virialised system. Galaxies may also collide, and such violent encounters destroy an ordered disc and leave behind exactly this kind of pressure-supported system. Hubble classified galaxy morphology a century ago, and the origin of that morphology remains one of the biggest open questions in astronomy.

Supermassive black holes & AGN feedback

Most galaxies in our Universe harbour a supermassive black hole (SMBH) at their centre. These black holes are fed by gas that funnels inward to the galactic nucleus, and so they grow in step with their host galaxies. Although a black hole is orders of magnitude smaller than the galaxy around it, it can deposit an enormous amount of energy into that galaxy, expelling gas and altering the whole course of its subsequent evolution — rather like a burp after a feast. A burping black hole renders the centre of its galaxy extremely luminous and extremely hot, a state observers term an active galactic nucleus (AGN); the process by which that energy is deposited is known as AGN feedback. Because it spans so many orders of magnitude in scale, understanding how a supermassive black hole grows alongside its host and how AGN feedback shapes galaxy evolution remains one of the most challenging problems in astronomy.

Cosmic Web

Galaxies tend to congregate, drawn together by their mutual gravitational attraction. That gravity is dominated by dark matter, whose total mass in our Universe exceeds that of the ordinary matter composed of atoms by roughly a factor of five. Gravity does not merely gather galaxies together, however: it arranges them into a magnificent spiderweb-like structure known as the cosmic web, comprising empty voids, wall-like sheets, filaments, and nodes. These configurations originate in the anisotropic collapse of structure under gravity, on scales tens to hundreds of times that of an individual halo. Where collapse has proceeded along all three spatial dimensions, the result is a node; where it has proceeded along two, leaving the third uncollapsed, a filament; along one alone, a sheet; and where no dimension has collapsed and matter is instead drawn outward by the surrounding structures, a void.

The cosmic web is built from individual dark matter haloes, and it in turn influences their formation histories and internal structure — a dependence known as halo assembly bias. Computer simulations of structure formation have greatly advanced our understanding of how the cosmic web and its constituent haloes interact. How these mechanisms shape the evolution and observable properties of the galaxies within those haloes, however, remains an open question, and one I am actively pursuing.
