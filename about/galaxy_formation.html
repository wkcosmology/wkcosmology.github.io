Galaxy Formation & Evolution

A galaxy's history can be described from four vantage points that refer to the same object. The cosmic web sets the initial conditions, channelling gas along filaments into growing dark matter halos whose assembly history is imprinted on the halo and, through it, on the galaxy it hosts. That accreted gas is processed by the baryon cycle: converted into stars, enriched with metals, and partly ejected and recycled through stellar and AGN feedback. In the most massive systems, the growth of a central supermassive black hole and its AGN feedback becomes the dominant regulator of that cycle, capable of shutting down star formation almost entirely. And the cumulative record of all of it — how much gas arrived, how it was processed, and when and how feedback intervened — is written into a galaxy's morphology: its size, and whether it remains a rotating disc or is transformed into a dispersion-supported spheroid. My work moves between all four of these rather than remaining within any one of them.

Underlying all four is a common question: whether a given galaxy formation model, semi-analytic or hydrodynamical, reproduces how real galaxies assemble, and, when two model classes disagree, which ingredient is responsible. This question does not belong to any single one of the four themes above; it is what motivates comparing them against each other.

  • Do different classes of galaxy formation model agree on how galaxies assemble, and when they disagree, why?
  • Which observed relation most tightly constrains the specific feedback ingredient a model needs to get right?
