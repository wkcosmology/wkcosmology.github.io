Galaxy Formation & Evolution

A galaxy's history can be told from four vantage points that describe the same object. It begins in the cosmic web, where the pattern of filaments and nodes determines where dark matter halos form and how quickly they grow; the galaxy that condenses at the centre of a halo inherits that assembly history, in its angular momentum, its accretion rate and the environment it sits in. The gas delivered along those filaments is then processed by the baryon cycle: converted into stars, enriched with the metals those stars synthesise, and partly ejected and later recycled through stellar and AGN feedback. Galaxies do not emerge from this process looking alike. They span a wide range of morphologies, from thin rotating discs to dispersion-supported spheroids, and that diversity is not incidental: a galaxy's size and structure record how much gas arrived, how it was processed and when it was disturbed, while in turn governing how efficiently the remaining gas is turned into stars. In the most massive systems the central supermassive black hole becomes the dominant regulator of the whole chain, its AGN feedback capable of starving a galaxy of fuel and shutting down star formation almost entirely, leaving behind the quiescent spheroids that populate the massive end of the population today. My work moves between all four of these for a comprehensive view of galaxy formation and evolution.
