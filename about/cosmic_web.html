Cosmic Web & Dark matter halo

The large-scale structure (LSS) of our Universe grows from a nearly uniform density field carrying small perturbations. Gravity amplifies these perturbations until they collapse into virialized dark matter haloes, which provide the deep potential wells where galaxies form. The haloes, and the galaxies they host, in turn trace the matter density field on large scales, the Cosmic Web. Halo growth is hierarchical: small haloes form first and later merge into larger ones. Galaxy formation and evolution follow this build-up closely, a coupling that manifests as the tight galaxy–halo connection. Quantifying the galaxy–halo–LSS connection has been a central task in astrophysics for decades.

The galaxy-halo connection is not trivial. Haloes of the same mass differ in concentration, spin, formation time and local environment, and these differences correlate both with the clustering of haloes, which is known as the assembly bias, and with the properties of the galaxies inside them.

The key questions I address are:

  • How are the assembly, structure, and spatial distribution of dark matter haloes related to each other?
  • How do halo properties beyond halo mass shape the properties of the galaxies?
  • How do observed galaxies trace the underlying density field?

I approach these questions by combining analytic models, semi-analytic galaxy formation models and cosmological hydrodynamical simulations. Analytic models isolate the physical mechanism at work and make its parameter dependence explicit; semi-analytic models place that mechanism on realistic halo merger trees and follow a full galaxy population; hydrodynamical simulations supply the resolved gas physics against which the simpler models are tested. Applied together with clustering and higher-order statistics, this hierarchy of models lets me connect the properties of an individual galaxy to the assembly history of its halo and to the cosmic web it inhabits.
