Baryon Cycle

A galaxy forms by accreting gas from the surrounding cosmic web, converting that gas into stars, and returning much of the mass to the interstellar medium (ISM) together with the metals the stars synthesise. Supernovae then drive enriched gas out of the galaxy, and in some cases out of the halo entirely, though a fraction of it later returns to fuel further star formation. This loop of accretion, star formation, ejection and recycling is the baryon cycle, and it governs how galaxies form and evolve. Each process leaves a distinct fingerprint on the chemical composition of the ISM and of the stellar component, which makes metallicity a powerful probe of the cycle. Those fingerprints surface observationally as various scaling relations of galaxies obey.

Decoding that fingerprint requires a model, because metallicity is set by several coupled processes acting at once. Cosmological hydrodynamical simulations and modern semi-analytic models treat those processes in detail, but they have grown sophisticated enough that the dependence of a given observable on a given physical process is no longer transparent. In practice they are used mainly for comparison with observational data, and such a comparison cuts neither way: agreement with an observed relation does not by itself identify the mechanism producing it, and disagreement cannot readily be traced to the failure of any particular process.

Analytic formulations make the opposite trade. They reduce the galaxy to a few coupled reservoirs, and in doing so retain the transparency needed to identify what drives a given observable. Used together, the two approaches cover each other's blind spots, and this pairing is the route I take to interpreting observations and decoding the baryon cycle. The key questions I address are:

  • What drives the chemical evolution of galaxies across cosmic time?
  • What is the role of feedback in shaping galaxy properties?
  • What drives star formation and its redshift evolution?
