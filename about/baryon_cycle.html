Baryon Cycle

Galaxy formation begins with the accretion of gas from the surrounding cosmic web, proceeds through the conversion of that gas into stars, and returns a substantial fraction of the mass to the interstellar medium (ISM) together with the metals synthesised in stellar interiors. Supernovae subsequently drive enriched gas out of the galaxy, and in some cases beyond the virial radius altogether, although a fraction is later reaccreted and fuels further star formation. This cycle of accretion, star formation, ejection and recycling — the baryon cycle — constitutes the foundation of the modern galaxy formation paradigm, and characterising it remains among the central questions in extragalactic astrophysics.

Despite the clarity of this physical picture, quantifying the individual processes observationally remains formidably difficult, and constraining the baryon cycle in real galaxies is correspondingly hard. Each process, however, imprints a distinct signature on the chemical composition of the ISM and of the stellar population, which renders metallicity a uniquely powerful diagnostic of the baryon cycle.

The baryon cycle is tightly regulated, as evidenced by the several close scaling relations that galaxy observables obey. Three are primary: the star-forming main sequence (SFMS), relating specific star formation rate — the ratio of star formation rate to stellar mass — to stellar mass; the gas-to-stellar mass relation, relating gas fraction to stellar mass; and the mass–metallicity relation (MZR), relating gas-phase metallicity to stellar mass. These three are themselves mutually correlated, and those correlations manifest as a set of higher-order relations: the fundamental metallicity relation (FMR), the gaseous FMR, and the fundamental plane of star formation. Because such relations encode a wealth of information about the baryon cycle, they have been measured extensively across cosmic time, from the local Universe out to the earliest epochs accessible to observation, and studied equally extensively in galaxy formation models, including semi-analytic models and hydrodynamical simulations.

A unified account of these relations nonetheless remains elusive. The principal obstacle is the complexity of contemporary galaxy formation models, which largely confines analysis to comparison between observed results and model outputs: a discrepancy cannot readily be traced to any particular ingredient, and an agreement offers little insight into which physics is responsible for it.

My approach to this problem is deliberately multi-level. I begin with the most minimal, most transparent model capable of addressing a given scaling relation. Where it succeeds, I validate it against more realistic models in which a far wider range of properties — many of them observationally inaccessible — can be examined. Where it fails, the transparecy makes the failure itself informative: it indicates that some ingredient is missing, and a targeted investigation of hydrodynamical simulations can identify what that ingredient is. This philosophy underpins my inflow-driven galaxy evolution series of papers.
