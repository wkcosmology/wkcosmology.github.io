Baryon Cycle

A galaxy forms by accreting gas from the surrounding cosmic web, converting that gas into stars, and returning much of the mass to the interstellar medium (ISM) together with the metals the stars synthesise. Supernovae following star formation expel enriched ISM out of the galaxy, and in some cases out of the halo entirely. This loop of accretion, star formation and ejection is the baryon cycle, and it governs how galaxies form and evolve. Each process leaves a distinct fingerprint on the chemical composition of the ISM and of the stellar component, which makes metallicity a powerful probe of the cycle. The fingerprints appear observationally as the scaling relations galaxies obey: the star-forming main sequence, the gas-to-stellar mass relation and the mass–metallicity relation, along with higher-order relations such as the fundamental metallicity relation, its gaseous form, and the fundamental plane of star formation.

Decoding the chemical fingerprint requires a model, because many coupled processes set the metallicity together. Cosmological hydrodynamical simulations solve the gas dynamics from first principles, but they rely on sub-grid prescriptions for star formation and for stellar and AGN feedback, each carrying its own tunable parameters. This buys realism at the cost of transparency: when a simulation reproduces an observed relation, or fails to, it is often not possible to identify which of the coupled processes is responsible.

Analytic formulations make the opposite trade. They simplify the galaxy to a few coupled reservoirs and retain the transparency needed to identify what drives a given observable. Combining realistic hydrodynamical simulations with analytic models therefore offers a complementary route to interpreting observations and decoding the baryon cycle. The key questions I address are:

  • What drives the chemical evolution of galaxies across cosmic time?
  • What is the quantitative role of feedback in setting that evolution?
